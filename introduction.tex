\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

The use of superconducting circuits as elements of a controllable
quantum system has, over the last twenty-five years, evolved from
the demonstration of a single Cooper-pair box~\cite{nakamura1999}
to multi-qubit processors operating at the threshold of practical
fault tolerance~\cite{arute2019,acharya2023}. The conceptual
ingredients that made this trajectory possible were assembled
gradually: the lumped-element quantisation of an electrical
circuit~\cite{devoret1995,vool2017}, the recognition that the
Josephson junction~\cite{josephson1962} provides the only known
nonlinear, non-dissipative inductor at low temperatures, the
design of a charge-noise-insensitive ``transmon''
qubit~\cite{koch2007}, and the realisation that coupling such a
qubit strongly to a microwave resonator implements an on-chip
analogue of cavity quantum electrodynamics --- circuit QED, or
cQED~\cite{blais2004,wallraff2004,blais2021}. The result is a
hardware platform on which the toolkit of quantum optics,
originally developed for atom--photon interactions in the optical
band, is realised at gigahertz frequencies in a chip-scale, fully
lithographic system, with single-quantum sensitivity to its own
microwave environment.

Working in this field requires fluency in three different bodies
of knowledge that are usually taught separately. The first is the
postulates and operational machinery of non-relativistic quantum
mechanics, including density matrices, composite systems, and
time-dependent perturbation theory~\cite{nielsen2010}. The second
is quantum optics: quantisation of the electromagnetic field,
Fock and coherent states, the Jaynes--Cummings model, and the
dispersive regime that supports
qubit-resonator readout~\cite{walls2008,haroche2006,blais2021}.
The third is the methods of open-system dynamics: the
Born--Markov approximation, the Lindblad master equation, and
input--output theory, all needed to describe relaxation,
dephasing, and continuous monitoring~\cite{lindblad1976,gardiner2004,breuer2002}.
Excellent textbooks treat each subject individually, but a working
practitioner of cQED uses only a particular cross-cut of each ---
the parts that flow into the design and analysis of a specific
experiment. The purpose of these notes is to collect that
cross-cut in a single, self-contained document, and to do so in a
uniform pedagogical style.

The six parts of these notes follow the order in which the
material is typically used. Part~I sets the formalism: the
postulates of quantum mechanics, the Hilbert-space and operator
language, density matrices and partial traces, time-dependent
perturbation theory, and the rotating-wave approximation that
recurs in every drive calculation later in the
text~\cite{nielsen2010}. Part~II builds on this to describe open
quantum systems, deriving the Lindblad master
equation~\cite{lindblad1976}, the relaxation and dephasing
times $T_1, T_2, T_2^*$, and the input--output formalism that
mediates between the system and its
environment~\cite{gardiner2004,breuer2002}. Part~III treats
quantum optics in the form needed for cQED: quantisation of the
electromagnetic field, Fock and coherent states, the
Jaynes--Cummings model, the dispersive regime, the
photon-number-dependent AC Stark shift, and the Purcell
effect~\cite{haroche2006,walls2008,blais2021}. Part~IV specialises
to superconducting circuits proper, applying the lumped-element
quantisation procedure to the LC resonator and the Josephson
junction, working through the transmon~\cite{koch2007} and related
qubit architectures, and discussing dispersive readout, single-
and two-qubit gates, the basic calibration sequence
(resonator and qubit spectroscopy, Rabi, Ramsey), and the leading
decoherence channels~\cite{krantz2019,blais2021,vool2017}.
Part~V steps back from the hardware to introduce quantum
information processing as such: gate operations and the universal
gate set, the diagrammatic conventions used to draw quantum
circuits, fully-worked examples (quantum teleportation; the
distinction between stabiliser operations and the
``magic''~\cite{bravyi2005} that enables universal quantum
computation), brief notes on quantum cryptography (BB84, E91) and
communication, and from there into quantum error
correction~\cite{shor1995,steane1996}, the stabiliser
formalism~\cite{gottesman1997}, the surface code~\cite{fowler2012}
that has come to dominate the fault-tolerance literature, and the
bosonic-code variants relevant to cQED
hardware~\cite{michael2016}. Part~VI is a tour of a more recent
research direction in which the same hardware doubles as a
particle-physics front end~\cite{dixit2021,hochberg2021,linehan2024}:
superconducting circuits as single-quantum detectors of sub-GeV
dark matter, dark photons, and axions in mass bands inaccessible
to traditional cryogenic detectors. An appendix collects the
elementary mathematical definitions (sesquilinearity, completeness,
dual space, tensor product) that the main text uses without
re-deriving.

A few global conventions are in force throughout. Operators carry
a hat, $\op{O}$, even when no classical counterpart could
realistically be confused with them; we never absorb $\hbar$ into
``natural units'', so Hamiltonians built from angular frequencies
read $\hbar\omega\dots$, the canonical commutator is
$[\op{x},\op{p}]=i\hbar\,\id$, and time evolution is
$e^{-i\op{H}t/\hbar}$. The identity is rendered as
$\mathbf{1}\equiv\id$. Pauli operators are denoted
$\hat\sigma_x,\hat\sigma_y,\hat\sigma_z$, with the convention
$\hat\sigma_z\ket{0}=+\ket{0}$ so that $\ket{0}$ is the qubit
ground state; bosonic ladder operators
$\hat a,\hat a^\dagger$ satisfy
$[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=\id$. Each topic in the main text is
presented in a small number of structural blocks --- a
description of the phenomenon, a formal statement, a derivation,
and an example or pointer to an application --- which makes it
easy to skim what one already knows and slow down where one does
not. Each chapter ends with a boxed Chapter-takeaways summary that
collects the key concepts and formulas, and a set of ten
exercises with worked solutions that invite the reader to
recompute the central results in applied form.

Readers familiar with non-relativistic quantum mechanics may skim
Part~I, treating it as a reference for notation, and proceed
directly to Part~III. Readers new to the field are encouraged to
read in order: the parts are deliberately layered, with later
parts using results, notation and intuition built up
earlier. Comments, corrections, and suggestions are warmly invited
on the public repository,
\href{https://github.com/fewagner/quantum101}{github.com/fewagner/quantum101}.
