\chapter{Two canonical model systems}
\label{ch:model-systems}

\section*{Chapter overview}

This chapter solves two systems that, between them, model most of
the dynamics encountered in circuit QED: the quantum harmonic
oscillator and the two-level system (TLS). They are
\emph{idealisations}, not faithful descriptions of any physical
hardware --- but they are the building blocks out of which the more
realistic models in later chapters are assembled.

LC resonators, microwave cavities and transmission-line modes are,
in the lossless and linear limit, exact harmonic oscillators.
Superconducting \emph{qubits}, by contrast, are not strictly
two-level: they are weakly anharmonic oscillators with an infinite
ladder of energy levels, designed so that the
$\ket{0}\!\to\!\ket{1}$ transition frequency differs slightly from
$\ket{1}\!\to\!\ket{2}$. The TLS is the standard idealisation that
ignores all but the lowest two levels; it is accurate when drives
and couplings are slow and weak enough that population of $\ket{2}$
and beyond stays negligible. When such leakage matters --- for
instance during fast gates or strong drives --- the more honest
picture is the (anharmonic) oscillator, and one has to keep track
of the full ladder.

We will routinely move between the two. A transmon (\cref{ch:circuit-qed})
behaves as a TLS for slow control and as an anharmonic oscillator
when leakage is on the agenda. The toolbox developed in this
chapter --- ladder operators, the Pauli algebra, the Bloch sphere,
and Rabi oscillations --- is what we need in either limit. The
chapter ends by combining a TLS with a coherent drive, producing
the Rabi oscillations that underlie all single-qubit control.

\section{The quantum harmonic oscillator}
\label{sec:qho}

\begin{phenomenon}
A particle in a quadratic potential, an LC circuit, and a single
mode of the electromagnetic field all share the same equation of
motion: simple harmonic motion at a single frequency $\omega$. Their
quantum versions therefore share a single Hilbert space structure
and a single energy spectrum.
\end{phenomenon}

\begin{statement}[Harmonic-oscillator spectrum]
\label{stmt:qho-spectrum}
Let $\op{x}$ and $\op{p}$ be the position and momentum operators of
\cref{def:position-momentum}, satisfying
$[\op{x},\op{p}]=i\hbar\,\id$. The Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
\op{H} \;=\; \frac{\op{p}^2}{2m} + \tfrac12 m\omega^2 \op{x}^2
\end{equation}
has eigenvalues $E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$ for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$,
with non-degenerate eigenstates $\ket{n}$ that span the Hilbert
space.
\end{statement}

\paragraph{Derivation via ladder operators.}
Define the dimensionless ladder operators
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ladder-def}
\hat{a}   = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\Bigl(\op{x}+\frac{i}{m\omega}\op{p}\Bigr),
\qquad
\hat{a}^\dagger = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\Bigl(\op{x}-\frac{i}{m\omega}\op{p}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Using $[\op{x},\op{p}]=i\hbar\,\id$ one immediately obtains
\begin{equation}
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^\dagger] = \id,\qquad
\op{H} = \hbar\omega\bigl(\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} + \tfrac12\bigr).
\end{equation}
The number operator $\op{N}=\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$ commutes with $\op{H}$, so
they share an eigenbasis. From $[\op{N},\hat{a}]=-\hat{a}$ and
$[\op{N},\hat{a}^\dagger]=+\hat{a}^\dagger$
one finds that $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger$ shift the eigenvalue of
$\op{N}$ by $\mp 1$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ladder-action}
\hat{a}\ket{n} = \sqrt{n}\,\ket{n-1},
\qquad
\hat{a}^\dagger\ket{n} = \sqrt{n+1}\,\ket{n+1}.
\end{equation}
Positivity, $\bra{\psi}\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}\ket{\psi}\geq0$, forces $n\geq0$;
a unique ground state $\ket{0}$ with $\hat{a}\ket{0}=0$ exists, and the
remaining eigenstates are
$\ket{n}=(\hat{a}^\dagger)^n/\sqrt{n!}\,\ket{0}$.

\begin{example}[Photons in a cavity]
A single mode of the electromagnetic field, treated in
\cref{ch:em-quantization}, has exactly this Hamiltonian with
$\op{N}$ counting photons. The same algebra describes phonons in a
crystal and, with the right choice of variables, the LC oscillator
of a superconducting circuit (\cref{ch:circuit-qed}).
\end{example}

\paragraph{Transition.}
The harmonic oscillator gives us an infinite ladder of evenly spaced
levels. The opposite extreme --- a system with only two levels ---
is the second cornerstone we need. We treat it next, and along the
way introduce the term \emph{qubit} that we have, until now, been
careful to avoid.

\section{The two-level system and the Pauli algebra}
\label{sec:tls}

\begin{phenomenon}
Whenever a small subset of energy levels is well separated from
everything else, dynamics restricted to that subset can be modelled
by a Hamiltonian acting on a low-dimensional Hilbert space. The
simplest non-trivial case --- a single isolated transition --- is
the two-level system.
\end{phenomenon}

\begin{definition}[Qubit]
\label{def:qubit}
A \emph{qubit} (quantum bit) is a two-level quantum system used as
the physical realisation of a logical bit of quantum information.
The two basis states are written $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, with
$\ket{0}$ playing the role of the classical $0$. The term is
agnostic about the physical implementation: a qubit can be an
electron spin, a photon polarisation, the two lowest levels of a
transmon (\cref{ch:circuit-qed}), and so on. From here on we use
``qubit'' and ``two-level system'' interchangeably, defaulting to
``qubit'' when the system is being thought of as a unit of quantum
information rather than just a bare two-level Hamiltonian.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Pauli operators]
\label{def:pauli}
On $\hilbert=\C^2$ with basis $\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}$, define
\begin{equation}
\hat\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\hat\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\hat\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Together with $\id$ they form a basis of the Hermitian operators on
$\C^2$.
\end{definition}

The Pauli operators satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pauli-algebra}
\hat\sigma_i\hat\sigma_j = \delta_{ij}\,\id + i\epsilon_{ijk}\hat\sigma_k,
\qquad
[\hat\sigma_i,\hat\sigma_j] = 2i\epsilon_{ijk}\hat\sigma_k,
\qquad
\{\hat\sigma_i,\hat\sigma_j\} = 2\delta_{ij}\,\id.
\end{equation}
We also use the raising and lowering operators
$\hat\sigma_\pm = (\hat\sigma_x\pm i\hat\sigma_y)/2$, with
$\hat\sigma_+\ket{1}=\ket{0}$, $\hat\sigma_-\ket{0}=\ket{1}$ in the
convention $\hat\sigma_z\ket{0}=+\ket{0}$ used throughout these notes.

\begin{remark}[Geometric meaning: generators of rotations]
The Pauli operators are the \emph{generators} of rotations on the
qubit's Hilbert space about the three coordinate axes. Concretely,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pauli-rotation}
\hat{R}_i(\theta) \;\equiv\; e^{-i\theta\hat\sigma_i/2}
\;=\; \cos(\theta/2)\,\id - i\sin(\theta/2)\,\hat\sigma_i,
\qquad i\in\{x,y,z\},
\end{equation}
implements a rotation by angle $\theta$ about the $i$-axis: it
takes the Bloch vector $\bm{r}$ (\cref{sec:bloch}) to the rotated
vector $R_i(\theta)\bm{r}$. The Pauli operators themselves
($\theta\to\infty$ in some sense) are not rotations --- they have
eigenvalues $\pm1$ and square to $\id$ --- but they generate the
rotation group through \cref{eq:pauli-rotation}. The factor
$\frac12$ in the exponent reflects the spinor structure of $\C^2$:
a $2\pi$ rotation maps $\ket{\psi}\to-\ket{\psi}$ rather than back
to $\ket{\psi}$, and only a $4\pi$ rotation is the identity. This
geometric picture is what makes the Bloch sphere
(\cref{sec:bloch}) such a useful tool.
\end{remark}

\begin{statement}[Most general TLS Hamiltonian]
\label{stmt:tls-hamiltonian}
Up to an irrelevant additive constant, every Hermitian operator on
$\C^2$ has the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tls-h}
\op{H} \;=\; \tfrac{\hbar}{2}\,\bm{\Omega}\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}},
\end{equation}
for some real vector $\bm{\Omega}=(\Omega_x,\Omega_y,\Omega_z)\in\R^3$
of angular frequencies. (We pull $\hbar$ out explicitly so that the
components of $\bm{\Omega}$ have units of $1/\text{time}$.)
\end{statement}

The eigenvalues of \cref{eq:tls-h} are $\pm\hbar|\bm{\Omega}|/2$, and the
eigenvectors are aligned with $\pm\bm{\Omega}/|\bm{\Omega}|$ in the geometric
sense made precise next.

\paragraph{Transition.}
The vector $\bm{\Omega}$ in \cref{eq:tls-h} suggests a geometric
picture. That picture is the Bloch sphere, and it turns the algebra
of a TLS into elementary geometry on $S^2$.

% --------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The Bloch sphere}
\label{sec:bloch}

\paragraph{From state vectors to density matrices.}
A state vector $\ket{\psi}\in\C^2$ describes \emph{maximal}
knowledge of a qubit: we know its preparation exactly. In practice
preparation is rarely perfect, and we frequently want to talk about
a qubit whose state is only known up to a classical probability
distribution. The right object for that is the \emph{density
matrix}, which we define below in the qubit-specific form needed
here; the same object plays a central role in \cref{ch:composite},
where it is set up in full generality and where we will also see
that interaction with an environment naturally turns pure states
into mixed ones.

\begin{definition}[Single-qubit density matrix and purity]
\label{def:density-tls}
A \emph{density matrix} on $\C^2$ is a Hermitian, positive
semi-definite operator $\op{\rho}$ with $\Tr\op{\rho}=1$. Every
$\ket{\psi}\in\C^2$ of unit norm gives a density matrix
$\op{\rho}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}$, and any convex combination
$\op{\rho}=\sum_n p_n\,\ket{\psi_n}\bra{\psi_n}$ with $p_n\geq0$,
$\sum_n p_n=1$ is again one. The \emph{purity} of $\op{\rho}$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}(\op{\rho}) \;=\; \Tr\op{\rho}^2 \;\in\; [\tfrac12,\,1].
\end{equation}
We call $\op{\rho}$ \emph{pure} when
$\mathcal{P}(\op{\rho})=1$ and \emph{mixed} otherwise; one shows
(see \cref{ch:composite}) that
$\mathcal{P}(\op{\rho})=1$ holds if and only if $\op{\rho}$ is a
projector onto a single state vector,
$\op{\rho}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{statement}[Bloch representation]
\label{stmt:bloch}
Every density matrix on $\C^2$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bloch-rho}
\op{\rho} \;=\; \tfrac12\bigl(\id + \bm{r}\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}}\bigr),
\qquad
\bm{r}\in\R^3,\;|\bm{r}|\leq1.
\end{equation}
The map $\op{\rho}\leftrightarrow\bm{r}$ is bijective; the surface
$|\bm{r}|=1$ corresponds exactly to the pure states of
\cref{def:density-tls}, and the interior $|\bm{r}|<1$ to the mixed
ones.
\end{statement}

\begin{proof}
Any Hermitian operator on $\C^2$ expands in the basis
$\{\id,\hat\sigma_x,\hat\sigma_y,\hat\sigma_z\}$ as
$\op{\rho}=c_0\id+\bm{c}\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}}$ for some $c_0\in\R$
and $\bm{c}\in\R^3$. Trace-one fixes
$c_0=\tfrac12$, and writing $\bm{c}=\tfrac12\bm{r}$ puts
\cref{eq:bloch-rho} in standard form.
The two eigenvalues of $\op{\rho}$ are $\tfrac12(1\pm|\bm{r}|)$;
positivity ($\op{\rho}\geq0$) requires both to be non-negative,
giving $|\bm{r}|\leq1$. A direct computation using
$(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot\bm{r})^2=|\bm{r}|^2\,\id$ then yields
$\Tr\op{\rho}^2=\tfrac12(1+|\bm{r}|^2)$, so the purity condition
$\Tr\op{\rho}^2=1$ from \cref{def:density-tls} translates exactly
to $|\bm{r}|=1$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Pure states and the geometry of the sphere.}
In coordinates a pure state $\ket{\psi}=\cos(\theta/2)\ket{0}+
e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)\ket{1}$ has Bloch vector
$\bm{r}=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)$,
parametrising the unit sphere $S^2$. The action of \cref{eq:tls-h}
on $\bm{r}$ is precession around the axis $\bm{\Omega}$ at angular
speed $|\bm{\Omega}|$ (the $\hbar$ in \cref{eq:tls-h} cancels with
the $\hbar$ in $e^{-i\op{H}t/\hbar}$).

\begin{remark}[How a pure state becomes mixed]
Mixed states ($|\bm{r}|<1$) arise in two distinct but related ways.
The first is \emph{classical uncertainty in preparation}: if the
device produces $\ket{\psi_n}$ with probability $p_n$, the state
relevant for predictions is $\op{\rho}=\sum_n p_n\,\ket{\psi_n}\bra{\psi_n}$,
which has $|\bm{r}|<1$ unless all $p_n$ are concentrated on a
single state. The second is \emph{coupling to an environment}:
even if the joint state of qubit and environment is pure, the
qubit's reduced state (obtained by the partial trace
of \cref{ch:composite}) is in general mixed. This second mechanism
is the origin of decoherence in real hardware and is treated in
detail in Part~II (\cref{ch:open-systems}). On the Bloch sphere
the qubit's $\bm{r}$ accordingly contracts towards the origin
under noise; the rate of that contraction is what the relaxation
($T_1$) and dephasing ($T_2$) times measure.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Single-qubit gates correspond to rotations of $\bm{r}$ about an axis
$\hat{\bm{n}}$ by an angle $\phi$, implemented by the unitary
\begin{equation}
\hat{R}_{\hat{\bm{n}}}(\phi) = \exp\!\bigl(-i\tfrac{\phi}{2}\,\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}}\bigr)
= \cos(\phi/2)\,\id - i\sin(\phi/2)\,\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}}.
\end{equation}
This is the Hamiltonian-engineering identity we use to think about
gate calibration in \cref{sec:rabi}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Transition.}
The static spectrum of a TLS is now fully characterised. The
question of how a TLS \emph{responds to a coherent drive} is what
turns this static picture into a tool for controlling qubits.

\section{Rabi oscillations}
\label{sec:rabi}

\begin{phenomenon}
A qubit driven coherently at a frequency close to its level
spacing does not just sit in $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$: it cycles
back and forth between the two states at a rate set by the drive
amplitude. The frequency of this cycle --- the \emph{Rabi
frequency} --- is the basic quantity that any qubit calibration
aims to measure (\cref{sec:experimental-basics}), and the cycle
itself is what every single-qubit gate exploits.
\end{phenomenon}

Consider the driven-qubit Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:driven-tls}
\op{H}(t) = \frac{\hbar\omega_q}{2}\hat\sigma_z
          + \hbar\Omega\cos(\omega_d t)\,\hat\sigma_x,
\end{equation}
with qubit angular frequency $\omega_q$, drive (angular) frequency
$\omega_d$, and drive amplitude $\Omega$ (also an angular
frequency). This is the same physical setup as
\cref{ex:tls-rotating-frame} of \cref{ch:postulates}, now written
in the Pauli-operator language of \cref{def:pauli}: the
identification is $\op{H}_0\,(\text{from Ex.\,\ref{ex:tls-rotating-frame}})
\leftrightarrow\frac{\hbar\omega_q}{2}\hat\sigma_z$ (up to a
$\frac12$-Identity additive constant) and
$\ket{0}\bra{1}+\ket{1}\bra{0}\leftrightarrow\hat\sigma_x$.
\Cref{ex:tls-rotating-frame} stopped after deriving the
interaction-picture Hamiltonian and identifying its slow and fast
parts. Here we go one step further and \emph{also} cancel the
slow oscillation, ending up with a strictly time-independent
Hamiltonian whose dynamics is \cref{thm:rabi}.

\paragraph{Move to a rotating frame.}
The reason \cref{eq:driven-tls} looks intractable is the
$\cos(\omega_d t)$: a static Hamiltonian we can exponentiate, a
time-dependent one in general we cannot. The remedy is to switch
to a frame in which the explicit time dependence is gone.
Concretely, we look for a unitary $\op{U}_0(t)$ such that the
transformed state $\ket{\tilde\psi(t)}=\op{U}_0^\dagger(t)\ket{\psi(t)}$
obeys $i\hbar\,\partial_t\ket{\tilde\psi}=\op{H}_R\ket{\tilde\psi}$
with $\op{H}_R$ \emph{time-independent}. This is exactly the
interaction-picture construction of \cref{stmt:pictures}: the
Hamiltonian transforms as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:frame-transform}
\op{H} \;\longmapsto\;
\op{H}_R(t) \;=\; \op{U}_0^\dagger(t)\,\op{H}(t)\,\op{U}_0(t)
\;-\; i\hbar\,\op{U}_0^\dagger(t)\,\dot{\op{U}}_0(t),
\end{equation}
where the second term (the ``inertial'' or ``frame'' term) accounts
for the fact that we're working in a moving reference frame. Any
unitary is a legal choice; what makes the construction useful is
choosing $\op{U}_0$ to absorb as much of the time dependence as
possible.

\Cref{ex:tls-rotating-frame} chose $\op{U}_0$ generated by
$\op{H}_0$ itself, ending up in a frame rotating at the system
frequency $\omega_q$. The terms then oscillated at the detuning
$\omega_q-\omega_d$ (slow) and at $\omega_q+\omega_d$ (fast). Here
we make the slightly different --- and stronger --- choice of
rotating at the \emph{drive} frequency $\omega_d$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rabi-rotating-unitary}
\op{U}_0(t) \;=\; e^{-i\omega_d t\,\hat\sigma_z/2}.
\end{equation}
That choice is engineered to cancel the explicit $\cos(\omega_d t)$
in \cref{eq:driven-tls}; it leaves us with a time-\emph{independent}
Hamiltonian once the fast counter-rotating piece is dropped, which
\cref{ex:tls-rotating-frame} did not achieve.

\paragraph{Carry out the transformation.}
Differentiating \cref{eq:rabi-rotating-unitary} gives the inertial
term
\begin{equation}
-i\hbar\,\op{U}_0^\dagger\dot{\op{U}}_0
\;=\; -\frac{\hbar\omega_d}{2}\hat\sigma_z.
\end{equation}
Because $\op{U}_0$ commutes with $\hat\sigma_z$, the diagonal piece
of $\op{H}$ is unchanged by conjugation, and combining with the
inertial term yields
\begin{equation}
\op{U}_0^\dagger\,\frac{\hbar\omega_q}{2}\hat\sigma_z\,\op{U}_0
- \frac{\hbar\omega_d}{2}\hat\sigma_z
\;=\; \frac{\hbar\Delta}{2}\hat\sigma_z,
\qquad \Delta\equiv\omega_q-\omega_d.
\end{equation}
For the off-diagonal piece, use the same identity as in
\cref{ex:tls-rotating-frame},
\begin{equation}
\op{U}_0^\dagger\,\hat\sigma_\pm\,\op{U}_0
\;=\; e^{\pm i\omega_d t}\,\hat\sigma_\pm,
\quad\text{hence}\quad
\op{U}_0^\dagger\,\hat\sigma_x\,\op{U}_0
\;=\; e^{+i\omega_d t}\hat\sigma_+ + e^{-i\omega_d t}\hat\sigma_-.
\end{equation}
Multiplying by
$\cos(\omega_d t)=\tfrac12(e^{i\omega_d t}+e^{-i\omega_d t})$ gives
four terms,
\begin{equation}
\hbar\Omega\cos(\omega_d t)\,\op{U}_0^\dagger\hat\sigma_x\op{U}_0
\;=\; \tfrac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\bigl[\hat\sigma_+ + \hat\sigma_- \bigr]
\;+\; \tfrac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\bigl[e^{+2i\omega_d t}\hat\sigma_+ + e^{-2i\omega_d t}\hat\sigma_-\bigr],
\end{equation}
of which the first bracket is the time-independent
\emph{co-rotating} piece (it equals
$\tfrac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_x$) and the second oscillates at
$\pm 2\omega_d$. Dropping the latter --- the rotating-wave
approximation of \cref{sec:rwa}, valid for
$\Omega\ll\omega_q+\omega_d$ --- finally yields the static
rotating-frame Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rabi-h-rwa}
\op{H}_R \;=\; \frac{\hbar\Delta}{2}\hat\sigma_z + \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_x.
\end{equation}
This is exactly the form \cref{eq:tls-h} with
$\bm{\Omega}=(\Omega,0,\Delta)$, so the Bloch vector precesses about
$\bm{\Omega}$ at the \emph{generalised Rabi frequency}
\begin{equation}
\Omega_R = \sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Rabi formula]
\label{thm:rabi}
For the driven TLS \cref{eq:driven-tls}, in the rotating-wave
approximation with the qubit initially in $\ket{0}$, the probability
of finding it in $\ket{1}$ at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rabi-formula}
P_{0\to 1}(t) \;=\; \frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\,
\sin^2\!\Bigl(\frac{\Omega_R\,t}{2}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The unitary generated by $\op{H}_R$ over a time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
e^{-i\op{H}_R t/\hbar}
= \cos(\Omega_R t/2)\,\id - i\sin(\Omega_R t/2)\,\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}},
\end{equation}
with $\hat{\bm{n}}=(\Omega,0,\Delta)/\Omega_R$ (the explicit factors of
$\hbar$ in $\op{H}_R$ and in the Schr\"odinger evolution cancel). Acting
on $\ket{0}$ and projecting onto $\ket{1}$ gives \cref{eq:rabi-formula}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Rabi calibration of a qubit]
On resonance $\Delta=0$ and $P_{0\to1}(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$. A
$\pi$-pulse, which inverts $\ket{0}\leftrightarrow\ket{1}$, requires
$\Omega t=\pi$. Sweeping the drive amplitude (or duration) and
fitting the resulting oscillation in $P_{0\to1}$ is the standard
``Rabi experiment'' used to calibrate the amplitude of single-qubit
gates in superconducting hardware (\cref{sec:experimental-basics}).
\end{example}

\section*{Chapter summary and outlook}

We have solved the two systems that recur throughout the rest of the
text. The harmonic oscillator gave us the ladder operators, the Fock
basis, and the equally spaced spectrum that defines a linear
resonator. The two-level system gave us the Pauli algebra, the
geometric picture of the Bloch sphere, and Rabi oscillations under
a coherent drive.

So far our quantum systems have been completely isolated and
described by a single state vector. Real experiments are not isolated:
the qubit and resonator are coupled, the system is entangled with
its environment, and the experimenter has only partial information
about its state. The next chapter introduces the language --- tensor
products, density matrices, and the partial trace --- that is needed
to handle composite and mixed states without giving up any of the
machinery developed so far.
