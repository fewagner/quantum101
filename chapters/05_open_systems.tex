\chapter{Open-system dynamics and the Lindblad master equation}
\label{ch:open-systems}

Every system treated so far has been \emph{closed}: its state
evolved unitarily under a Hamiltonian, and the Schrödinger
equation determined the dynamics for all time. No real device is
closed. A superconducting qubit is capacitively coupled to control
lines, radiates into its electromagnetic environment, exchanges
energy with two-level defects in its oxide, and is bombarded by
quasiparticles and stray photons. The honest description keeps the
qubit \emph{and} its environment in one enormous closed system, but
we never want --- and never have --- the environment's state. What
we want is an equation for the qubit alone. This chapter derives
that equation. Starting from the reduced density operator
(\cref{ch:composite}), we make the two approximations --- weak
coupling and short bath memory --- that turn the intractable exact
dynamics into a time-local equation of motion, and we arrive at the
Lindblad master equation, the workhorse of open-system dynamics in
cavity and circuit QED. The relaxation and dephasing times of
\cref{ch:relaxation-dephasing}, the input--output relations of
\cref{ch:input-output}, and the continuous-measurement
unravellings of \cref{ch:continuous-measurement} are all read off
from it.

\section{The reduced state of an open system}
\label{sec:reduced-state}

\begin{phenomenon}
A system $S$ in contact with an environment (or ``bath'') $B$
cannot be assigned a state vector of its own: $S$ and $B$ generally
become entangled, and the only complete description is the joint
state $\op\rho_{SB}$. The experimentally accessible object is the
\emph{reduced} state $\op\rho_S=\Tr_B\op\rho_{SB}$, obtained by
tracing out the inaccessible bath. Even when the joint evolution is
unitary, the reduced evolution is not: probability flows
coherently between $S$ and $B$, so $\op\rho_S$ generically loses
purity, and the pure-state Schrödinger equation no longer applies.
\end{phenomenon}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We split the total Hamiltonian of the closed $S+B$ universe into
three pieces,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sb-hamiltonian}
\op H \;=\; \op H_S \otimes \id_B \;+\; \id_S \otimes \op H_B
\;+\; \op H_{SB},
\end{equation}
with $\op H_S$ acting on the system Hilbert space $\hilbert_S$,
$\op H_B$ on the (typically infinite-dimensional) bath space
$\hilbert_B$, and the interaction $\op H_{SB}$ coupling the two. It
is convenient to write the interaction in the separable form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sb-interaction}
\op H_{SB} \;=\; \hbar\sum_\alpha \op S_\alpha \otimes \op B_\alpha,
\end{equation}
where $\op S_\alpha$ are system operators and $\op B_\alpha$ bath
operators (any interaction can be brought to this form by expanding
in operator bases). The total state evolves unitarily,
$\op\rho_{SB}(t)=\op U(t)\,\op\rho_{SB}(0)\,\op U^\dagger(t)$ with
$\op U=e^{-i\op H t/\hbar}$, and the reduced state is recovered at
each instant by the partial trace of \cref{def:partial-trace},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced-state}
\op\rho_S(t) \;=\; \Tr_B\bigl[\op U(t)\,\op\rho_{SB}(0)\,\op U^\dagger(t)\bigr].
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Why this is not a closed equation.}
\Cref{eq:reduced-state} is exact but useless as it stands: it
requires propagating the full $S+B$ state and only then discarding
$B$. The decoherence example of
\cref{ex:decoherence-by-partial-trace} was a special case in which
the partial trace could be done by hand because the interaction was
diagonal and the bath trivial. In general we cannot. The goal of
the chapter is to find, under controlled approximations, a
\emph{self-contained} equation $\dot{\op\rho}_S=\mathcal L\op\rho_S$
in which the bath appears only through a few numbers (rates and
frequency shifts), not as a dynamical partner.

\paragraph{The interaction picture.}
We work in the interaction picture with respect to the free
Hamiltonian $\op H_0=\op H_S+\op H_B$ (the picture transformation of
\cref{stmt:pictures}), writing
$\tilde{\op\rho}_{SB}(t)=e^{i\op H_0 t/\hbar}\,\op\rho_{SB}(t)\,e^{-i\op H_0 t/\hbar}$.
The von Neumann equation for the joint state becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:vn-interaction}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\tilde{\op\rho}_{SB}(t)
\;=\; -\frac{i}{\hbar}\,\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t)}{\tilde{\op\rho}_{SB}(t)},
\end{equation}
with $\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t)=e^{i\op H_0 t/\hbar}\op H_{SB}\,e^{-i\op H_0 t/\hbar}$
the interaction-picture coupling. Everything that follows is an
approximate solution of \cref{eq:vn-interaction} for the reduced
state $\tilde{\op\rho}_S=\Tr_B\tilde{\op\rho}_{SB}$.

\paragraph{Transition.}
The exact equation \cref{eq:vn-interaction} still entangles $S$
with $B$. To make progress we need physical assumptions about the
bath --- that it is large, weakly coupled, and quick to forget. The
next section states these assumptions precisely.

\section{The Born--Markov approximations}
\label{sec:born-markov}

\begin{phenomenon}
A useful environment is one that is \emph{big}, \emph{weakly
coupled}, and \emph{forgetful}. Big: the bath has so many degrees
of freedom that the small amount of energy and information it
absorbs from the system never measurably changes its own state.
Weakly coupled: the interaction $\op H_{SB}$ is small enough to
treat to second order. Forgetful: bath correlations decay on a
timescale $\tau_B$ much shorter than the timescale $\tau_S$ over
which the system state changes appreciably. These three statements,
made precise, are the Born and Markov approximations.
\end{phenomenon}

\paragraph{Formal integration and the Born approximation.}
Integrate \cref{eq:vn-interaction} once and resubstitute it into
itself to get an equation exact to second order in $\op H_{SB}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:second-order}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\tilde{\op\rho}_{SB}(t)
= -\frac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t)}{\op\rho_{SB}(0)}
- \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\!\int_0^t\!\!\dd t'\,
\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t)}{\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t')}{\tilde{\op\rho}_{SB}(t')}}.
\end{equation}
Now trace over the bath. The \emph{Born approximation} asserts that,
because the bath is large and the coupling weak, the joint state
stays approximately factorised with the bath frozen in a stationary
state $\op\rho_B$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:born-factorisation}
\tilde{\op\rho}_{SB}(t) \;\approx\; \tilde{\op\rho}_S(t)\otimes\op\rho_B,
\end{equation}
and that $\op\rho_B$ commutes with $\op H_B$ (it is a thermal or
vacuum state). Choosing the coupling operators so that
$\Tr_B[\op B_\alpha\,\op\rho_B]=0$ (any non-zero mean is absorbed
into $\op H_S$), the first term of \cref{eq:second-order} drops out
of the bath trace, leaving the second-order \emph{Redfield}
equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:redfield}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\tilde{\op\rho}_S(t)
= -\frac{1}{\hbar^2}\!\int_0^t\!\!\dd t'\;
\Tr_B\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t)}{\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t')}{\tilde{\op\rho}_S(t')\otimes\op\rho_B}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Bath correlation functions.}
Carrying out the bath trace in \cref{eq:redfield} produces the
\emph{bath correlation functions}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bath-correlation}
C_{\alpha\beta}(t-t') \;=\; \Tr_B\bigl[\tilde{\op B}_\alpha(t)\,\tilde{\op B}_\beta(t')\,\op\rho_B\bigr],
\end{equation}
which --- because $\op\rho_B$ is stationary --- depend only on the
time difference $t-t'$. The whole influence of the bath on the
system is encoded in these functions. Physically, $C_{\alpha\beta}$
measures how long the bath ``remembers'' a kick from the system.

\begin{statement}[\textbf{Born--Markov approximation}]
\label{stmt:born-markov}
Assume the bath correlation functions \cref{eq:bath-correlation}
decay on a timescale $\tau_B$ much shorter than the system
relaxation timescale $\tau_S$ over which $\tilde{\op\rho}_S$
evolves:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:markov-condition}
\tau_B \;\ll\; \tau_S.
\end{equation}
Then two simplifications of the Redfield equation
\cref{eq:redfield} are justified. First (the \emph{Markov}
replacement), since $C_{\alpha\beta}(t-t')$ is sharply peaked at
$t'=t$, the slowly varying system state may be evaluated at the
present time, $\tilde{\op\rho}_S(t')\to\tilde{\op\rho}_S(t)$,
removing the memory integral. Second, the lower limit of the
time integral may be extended to $-\infty$ because the integrand
has no support beyond $t'\approx t-\tau_B$. The result is a
\emph{time-local} equation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:born-markov-eq}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\tilde{\op\rho}_S(t)
= -\frac{1}{\hbar^2}\!\int_0^{\infty}\!\!\dd\tau\;
\Tr_B\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t)}{\comm{\tilde{\op H}_{SB}(t-\tau)}{\tilde{\op\rho}_S(t)\otimes\op\rho_B}},
\end{equation}
in which the future of $\op\rho_S$ depends only on its present, not
its history.
\end{statement}

\paragraph{What the two timescales mean physically.}
For a superconducting qubit at $\omega_q/2\pi\sim 5\,$GHz coupled to
a $50\,\Omega$ electromagnetic environment, the bath correlation
time $\tau_B$ is set by the inverse bandwidth over which the
environmental spectral density is flat --- picoseconds to
nanoseconds --- whereas $\tau_S\sim T_1\sim 100\,\mu$s is the
relaxation time. The separation $\tau_B\ll\tau_S$ spans many
orders of magnitude, which is exactly why the Markovian description
is so accurate in cQED. When it fails --- a sharply structured
environment such as a single near-resonant two-level defect, or a
narrow-band filter cavity --- one must either enlarge the system to
absorb the offending mode or use a non-Markovian formalism.

\paragraph{Transition.}
\Cref{eq:born-markov-eq} is time-local but not yet in its canonical
form, and as written it does not manifestly preserve the positivity
of $\op\rho_S$. A final rearrangement --- the secular
approximation --- brings it to the unique form that does.

\section{The Lindblad master equation}
\label{sec:lindblad}

\begin{statement}[\textbf{Lindblad / GKLS master equation}~\cite{lindblad1976,gorini1976}]
\label{stmt:lindblad}
Under the Born--Markov approximation (\cref{stmt:born-markov})
followed by the secular approximation (dropping cross terms that
oscillate at the system's Bohr frequency differences), the reduced
density operator of an open system obeys, back in the Schrödinger
picture,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lindblad}
\frac{\dd\op\rho}{\dd t}
\;=\; -\frac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\op H}{\op\rho}
\;+\; \sum_k \gamma_k\,\mathcal D[\op L_k]\,\op\rho,
\end{equation}
where $\op H=\op H_S+\op H_{\rm LS}$ is the system Hamiltonian
augmented by a bath-induced \emph{Lamb shift} $\op H_{\rm LS}$
(which commutes with $\op H_S$), the $\op L_k$ are the
\emph{jump operators}, $\gamma_k\geq 0$ are the associated rates,
and the \emph{dissipator} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dissipator}
\mathcal D[\op L]\,\op\rho
\;=\; \op L\,\op\rho\,\op L^\dagger
\;-\; \tfrac12\,\op L^\dagger\op L\,\op\rho
\;-\; \tfrac12\,\op\rho\,\op L^\dagger\op L
\;=\; \op L\,\op\rho\,\op L^\dagger - \tfrac12\acomm{\op L^\dagger\op L}{\op\rho}.
\end{equation}
This is the most general generator of a continuous, time-homogeneous
family of completely positive, trace-preserving maps. The first
term of \cref{eq:lindblad} is the familiar unitary (von Neumann)
evolution; the dissipator is the non-unitary part that captures
relaxation, dephasing, and decay.
\end{statement}

\paragraph{Derivation sketch.}
Insert the separable interaction \cref{eq:sb-interaction} into the
Born--Markov equation \cref{eq:born-markov-eq}. Decompose each
system operator $\op S_\alpha$ into eigenoperators of $\op H_S$,
$\op S_\alpha(\omega)=\sum_{\epsilon'-\epsilon=\hbar\omega}
\Pi(\epsilon)\,\op S_\alpha\,\Pi(\epsilon')$, where $\Pi(\epsilon)$
projects onto the eigenspace of $\op H_S$ at energy $\epsilon$;
these satisfy $\tilde{\op S}_\alpha(\omega,t)=e^{-i\omega t}\op S_\alpha(\omega)$.
The bath trace turns the correlation functions into their
one-sided Fourier transforms
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gamma-transform}
\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
\;=\; \int_0^\infty\!\!\dd\tau\; e^{i\omega\tau}\,C_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)
\;=\; \tfrac12\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega) + i\,S_{\alpha\beta}(\omega),
\end{equation}
whose real part $\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ is the bath
\emph{spectral density} at frequency $\omega$ (the rate) and whose
imaginary part $S_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ produces the Lamb shift.
The \emph{secular approximation} keeps only the terms in which the
two eigenoperators oscillate at the same frequency (the cross terms
oscillate at $e^{i(\omega-\omega')t}$ and average to zero when
$|\omega-\omega'|\gg \tau_S^{-1}$). Diagonalising the positive
matrix $\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ yields the rates $\gamma_k$
and the jump operators $\op L_k$, and the equation collapses to the
canonical form \cref{eq:lindblad}. A complete account is in
\cite{breuer2002}; the structural result --- that this is the
\emph{only} form a Markovian generator can take --- is the
Gorini--Kossakowski--Sudarshan--Lindblad theorem
\cite{lindblad1976,gorini1976}.

\paragraph{Reading the dissipator.}
The combination in \cref{eq:dissipator} is not arbitrary. The
``sandwich'' term $\op L\op\rho\op L^\dagger$ is the \emph{quantum
jump}: it takes the state and acts with $\op L$ on both sides, the
way a measurement outcome would update the state. The
anticommutator term $-\tfrac12\acomm{\op L^\dagger\op L}{\op\rho}$
is the \emph{no-jump} back-action that keeps the trace constant:
one verifies directly, using cyclicity of the trace, that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trace-preserving}
\Tr\bigl[\mathcal D[\op L]\op\rho\bigr]
= \Tr\bigl[\op L^\dagger\op L\,\op\rho\bigr]
- \tfrac12\Tr\bigl[\op L^\dagger\op L\,\op\rho\bigr]
- \tfrac12\Tr\bigl[\op\rho\,\op L^\dagger\op L\bigr]
= 0,
\end{equation}
so probability is conserved, $\tfrac{\dd}{\dd t}\Tr\op\rho=0$. The
two pieces are exactly balanced; weakening either one would let
probability leak.

\begin{example}[\textbf{Amplitude damping of a qubit}]
\label{ex:amplitude-damping}
The most important single instance of \cref{eq:lindblad} is a
two-level system that can emit a quantum into a zero-temperature
bath but not absorb one. Take $\op H=-\tfrac{\hbar\omega_q}{2}\hat\sigma_z$
and a single jump operator $\op L_1=\hat\sigma_-=\ket{0}\!\bra{1}$
with rate $\gamma_1=\gamma$ (the decay rate). The master equation
is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:amplitude-damping-me}
\frac{\dd\op\rho}{\dd t}
= -\frac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\op H}{\op\rho}
+ \gamma\Bigl(\hat\sigma_-\op\rho\,\hat\sigma_+
- \tfrac12\acomm{\hat\sigma_+\hat\sigma_-}{\op\rho}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Writing $\op\rho$ in the
$\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}$ basis with excited-state population
$\rho_{11}$ and coherence $\rho_{01}$, the dissipator gives
$\dot\rho_{11}=-\gamma\rho_{11}$ and
$\dot\rho_{01}=(i\omega_q-\tfrac{\gamma}{2})\rho_{01}$. The
excited-state population decays at rate $\gamma$ and the coherence
at rate $\gamma/2$ --- the population relaxation defines $T_1=1/\gamma$,
and the factor-of-two relation between population and coherence
decay is the seed of the $T_1$--$T_2$ connection developed
systematically in \cref{ch:relaxation-dephasing}. This is the exact
Markovian counterpart of the spontaneous-emission rate computed
from Fermi's golden rule in \cref{ch:perturbations}.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Transition.}
We now have a closed, positivity-preserving equation of motion for
any open Markovian system. Before specialising it to the qubit
relaxation and dephasing of the next chapter, it is worth pausing
on \emph{why} the dissipator must take precisely the form
\cref{eq:dissipator} --- the answer is the requirement of complete
positivity.

\section{Complete positivity and the dynamical-map picture}
\label{sec:cptp}

\begin{phenomenon}
A physical evolution must map density operators to density
operators: it must preserve the trace (probabilities sum to one)
and positivity (no negative probabilities). It must do so even when
the system is entangled with an innocent bystander that is not
acted on at all. This last, stronger requirement --- \emph{complete}
positivity --- is what singles out the Lindblad form and rules out
otherwise plausible-looking decay equations.
\end{phenomenon}

\begin{statement}[\textbf{Dynamical maps and Kraus form}]
\label{stmt:kraus}
The finite-time evolution generated by \cref{eq:lindblad} is a
family of \emph{dynamical maps} $\op\rho(t)=\mathcal E_t[\op\rho(0)]$,
each of which is completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP)
and therefore admits a \emph{Kraus} (operator-sum) representation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kraus}
\mathcal E_t[\op\rho] \;=\; \sum_\mu \op K_\mu\,\op\rho\,\op K_\mu^\dagger,
\qquad \sum_\mu \op K_\mu^\dagger\op K_\mu = \id.
\end{equation}
Conversely, the Lindblad generator is recovered as the
infinitesimal limit: for a short time $\dd t$, the Kraus operators
are $\op K_0=\id-\bigl(\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\op H+\tfrac12\sum_k\gamma_k\op L_k^\dagger\op L_k\bigr)\dd t$
and $\op K_k=\sqrt{\gamma_k\,\dd t}\,\op L_k$, and substituting into
\cref{eq:kraus} reproduces \cref{eq:lindblad} to first order in
$\dd t$.
\end{statement}

\paragraph{Why complete positivity forbids naive decay laws.}
Consider ``decay'' modelled by simply shrinking the off-diagonal
coherences while doing nothing else --- a tempting but illegal
operation. Such a map can be positive on the system alone yet send
an entangled joint state to one with negative eigenvalues, an
unphysical result. The Lindblad dissipator avoids this because its
infinitesimal Kraus operators \cref{stmt:kraus} guarantee complete
positivity by construction. This is the operational content of the
GKLS theorem: among all time-local generators, exactly those of the
form \cref{eq:lindblad} with $\gamma_k\geq 0$ generate legal
quantum channels.

\paragraph{Connection to the measurement picture.}
The Kraus decomposition \cref{eq:kraus} is also the bridge to
continuous measurement. Reading $\op K_k=\sqrt{\gamma_k\dd t}\,\op L_k$
as the operator that updates the state \emph{given} that a quantum
was emitted into the bath in the interval $\dd t$, and
$\op K_0$ as the update \emph{given} that none was, turns the
master equation into a stochastic equation for individual
experimental runs. That unravelling is the subject of
\cref{ch:continuous-measurement}; the deterministic master equation
\cref{eq:lindblad} is what one recovers after averaging over all
measurement records.

This chapter built the central equation of open-system dynamics. We
began with the exact but intractable reduced state
\cref{eq:reduced-state}, introduced the Born approximation
(factorised state, weak coupling) and the Markov approximation
(short bath memory) to obtain a time-local equation, and applied
the secular approximation to bring it to the canonical Lindblad
form \cref{eq:lindblad}. The dissipator's exact structure is fixed
by complete positivity, equivalently by the Kraus representation of
the finite-time dynamical map.

The Lindblad equation is the foundation for the rest of Part~II. In
\cref{ch:relaxation-dephasing} we choose the jump operators
$\hat\sigma_-$ and $\hat\sigma_z$ to read off the relaxation and
dephasing times of a qubit; in \cref{ch:input-output} we recover
the same dissipation from the explicit dynamics of a bosonic bath
and obtain the input--output boundary relations; and in
\cref{ch:continuous-measurement} we unravel the equation into the
quantum trajectories that describe a single, continuously monitored
device.

\begin{chaptersummary}

\paragraph{Reduced state.}
A system coupled to a bath has no state vector of its own; its
description is the reduced density operator
$\op\rho_S=\Tr_B\op\rho_{SB}$, whose evolution is non-unitary even
when the joint $S+B$ evolution is unitary.

\paragraph{Born--Markov.}
For a large, weakly coupled, short-memory bath ($\tau_B\ll\tau_S$),
the reduced dynamics becomes time-local: the Born approximation
factorises the joint state $\op\rho_{SB}\approx\op\rho_S\otimes\op\rho_B$
and works to second order in the coupling, while the Markov
approximation evaluates the system at the present time and extends
the memory integral to infinity. The bath enters only through the
correlation functions $C_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)$ and their transforms,
whose real parts are rates and imaginary parts the Lamb shift.

\paragraph{Lindblad master equation.}
The unique generator of a continuous CPTP family is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd\op\rho}{\dd t}
= -\frac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\op H}{\op\rho}
+ \sum_k\gamma_k\Bigl(\op L_k\op\rho\op L_k^\dagger
- \tfrac12\acomm{\op L_k^\dagger\op L_k}{\op\rho}\Bigr),
\qquad \gamma_k\geq 0.
\end{equation}
The sandwich term is the quantum jump; the anticommutator term is
the no-jump back-action that preserves the trace.

\paragraph{Complete positivity.}
The finite-time map is CPTP, with Kraus form
$\mathcal E_t[\op\rho]=\sum_\mu\op K_\mu\op\rho\op K_\mu^\dagger$,
$\sum_\mu\op K_\mu^\dagger\op K_\mu=\id$. Infinitesimally,
$\op K_0=\id-(\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\op H+\tfrac12\sum_k\gamma_k\op L_k^\dagger\op L_k)\dd t$
and $\op K_k=\sqrt{\gamma_k\dd t}\,\op L_k$. Complete positivity is
what fixes the dissipator's form and forbids naive decay laws.

\paragraph{Canonical example.}
Amplitude damping: $\op L=\hat\sigma_-$, rate $\gamma$. The excited
population decays at $\gamma$ and the coherence at $\gamma/2$,
giving $T_1=1/\gamma$ and seeding the $T_1$--$T_2$ relation of
\cref{ch:relaxation-dephasing}.

\end{chaptersummary}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Non-unitarity from entangling dynamics]
Let $S$ and $B$ be single qubits, initially in the product state
$\ket{+}_S\otimes\ket{0}_B$ with $\ket{+}=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2$,
and let them evolve under a CNOT (control $S$, target $B$). Compute
$\op\rho_S$ before and after. Show that an initially pure system
state becomes mixed, even though the joint evolution is unitary.
\begin{solution}
Before: $\op\rho_S=\ket{+}\!\bra{+}$, pure ($\Tr\op\rho_S^2=1$).
The CNOT maps $\ket{+}\ket0=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00}+\ket{10})$ to
$\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})$, a Bell state. Tracing out $B$
gives $\op\rho_S=\tfrac12(\ket0\!\bra0+\ket1\!\bra1)=\tfrac12\id$,
maximally mixed ($\Tr\op\rho_S^2=\tfrac12$). The interaction
converted system purity into $S$--$B$ entanglement, the microscopic
origin of decoherence.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trace preservation of the dissipator]
Verify explicitly that $\Tr\bigl[\mathcal D[\op L]\op\rho\bigr]=0$
for any $\op L$ and any $\op\rho$, and that consequently the
Lindblad equation \cref{eq:lindblad} preserves $\Tr\op\rho$.
\begin{solution}
Using linearity and cyclicity of the trace,
$\Tr[\op L\op\rho\op L^\dagger]=\Tr[\op L^\dagger\op L\op\rho]$, so
$\Tr[\mathcal D[\op L]\op\rho]=\Tr[\op L^\dagger\op L\op\rho]
-\tfrac12\Tr[\op L^\dagger\op L\op\rho]-\tfrac12\Tr[\op\rho\op L^\dagger\op L]=0$.
The commutator term also has zero trace
($\Tr\comm{\op H}{\op\rho}=0$), so $\tfrac{\dd}{\dd t}\Tr\op\rho=0$.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Hermiticity preservation]
Show that if $\op\rho=\op\rho^\dagger$ at $t=0$, the Lindblad
generator keeps $\op\rho$ Hermitian for all $t$.
\begin{solution}
Take the adjoint of the right-hand side of \cref{eq:lindblad}. The
commutator term: $(-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\op H}{\op\rho})^\dagger
=+\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\op H}{\op\rho^\dagger}\cdot(-1)$... explicitly
$(-\tfrac i\hbar[\op H,\op\rho])^\dagger=\tfrac i\hbar[\op\rho^\dagger,\op H]
=-\tfrac i\hbar[\op H,\op\rho^\dagger]$, which equals the original
term when $\op\rho=\op\rho^\dagger$. The dissipator
\cref{eq:dissipator} is manifestly self-adjoint under
$\op\rho\to\op\rho^\dagger$ since $(\op L\op\rho\op L^\dagger)^\dagger
=\op L\op\rho^\dagger\op L^\dagger$ and the anticommutator is
self-adjoint. Hence $\dot{\op\rho}^\dagger=\dot{\op\rho}$ and
Hermiticity is preserved.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Steady state of amplitude damping]
For \cref{eq:amplitude-damping-me}, find the stationary state
$\dot{\op\rho}=0$ and interpret it physically.
\begin{solution}
From $\dot\rho_{11}=-\gamma\rho_{11}$ the steady state has
$\rho_{11}=0$; from $\dot\rho_{01}=(i\omega_q-\gamma/2)\rho_{01}$ the
coherence also decays to zero. The stationary state is the ground
state $\ket0\!\bra0$. Physically, a zero-temperature bath can only
absorb energy, so the qubit relaxes to its ground state and stays
there.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Population and coherence decay rates]
From \cref{eq:amplitude-damping-me}, confirm that the excited
population decays at $\gamma$ while the coherence decays at
$\gamma/2$. Why is the coherence rate exactly half?
\begin{solution}
The jump term $\hat\sigma_-\op\rho\hat\sigma_+$ contributes only to
$\rho_{00}$; the anticommutator
$-\tfrac\gamma2\acomm{\hat\sigma_+\hat\sigma_-}{\op\rho}$ with
$\hat\sigma_+\hat\sigma_-=\ket1\!\bra1$ gives
$\dot\rho_{11}=-\gamma\rho_{11}$ and, for the off-diagonal element,
$-\tfrac\gamma2\rho_{01}$ (only the $\ket1$ side of $\rho_{01}$ is
hit). Coherence involves one factor of the excited amplitude, so it
sees half the population decay rate. This is the $1/T_2=1/(2T_1)$
floor in the absence of pure dephasing.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Markov condition for a transmon]
A transmon couples to a $50\,\Omega$ line whose noise spectrum is
flat over a bandwidth $\sim 1\,$GHz, giving $\tau_B\sim 1\,$ns.
Its relaxation time is $T_1=100\,\mu$s. Estimate the ratio
$\tau_B/\tau_S$ and comment on the validity of the Born--Markov
approximation. When might it break down?
\begin{solution}
$\tau_B/\tau_S\sim 10^{-9}/10^{-4}=10^{-5}$, so the timescale
separation \cref{eq:markov-condition} holds by five orders of
magnitude --- Born--Markov is excellent. It breaks down when the
environment is sharply structured: a near-resonant two-level
defect, a high-$Q$ filter cavity, or $1/f$ charge/flux noise with
long correlation times. Then one enlarges the system to include the
offending mode, or uses a non-Markovian formalism.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Kraus operators for amplitude damping]
Show that the finite-time amplitude-damping channel has Kraus
operators
$\op K_0=\ket0\!\bra0+\sqrt{1-p}\,\ket1\!\bra1$ and
$\op K_1=\sqrt{p}\,\ket0\!\bra1$ with $p=1-e^{-\gamma t}$, and
verify $\op K_0^\dagger\op K_0+\op K_1^\dagger\op K_1=\id$.
\begin{solution}
$\op K_1$ takes $\ket1$ to $\ket0$ with amplitude $\sqrt p$ (a
decay event); $\op K_0$ leaves $\ket0$ untouched and damps $\ket1$
by $\sqrt{1-p}$ (no decay, with back-action). Then
$\op K_0^\dagger\op K_0=\ket0\!\bra0+(1-p)\ket1\!\bra1$ and
$\op K_1^\dagger\op K_1=p\,\ket1\!\bra1$, summing to
$\ket0\!\bra0+\ket1\!\bra1=\id$. Applying $\mathcal E[\op\rho]
=\op K_0\op\rho\op K_0^\dagger+\op K_1\op\rho\op K_1^\dagger$
reproduces $\rho_{11}\to(1-p)\rho_{11}=e^{-\gamma t}\rho_{11}$ and
$\rho_{01}\to\sqrt{1-p}\,\rho_{01}=e^{-\gamma t/2}\rho_{01}$,
matching \cref{ex:amplitude-damping}.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A positive but not completely positive map]
The transpose map $\op\rho\mapsto\op\rho^{\sf T}$ is positive (it
preserves eigenvalues, hence positivity, of any single-system
state). Show it is \emph{not} completely positive by applying
$(\text{transpose})\otimes\text{id}$ to a Bell state and finding a
negative eigenvalue.
\begin{solution}
Take $\ket{\Phi^+}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})$, density
matrix with entries $\tfrac12$ at positions $00\,00$, $00\,11$,
$11\,00$, $11\,11$. Partial transpose on the first qubit swaps the
off-diagonal block, producing the matrix with $\tfrac12$ on the
diagonal $00,11$ and $\tfrac12$ swapping $01\leftrightarrow10$. Its
eigenvalues are $\{\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\}$ --- the
$-\tfrac12$ shows the partially transposed operator is not a valid
density matrix. Hence transpose is positive but not completely
positive, illustrating why the stronger condition is required of
physical maps (and why the Lindblad form, which is automatically
CP, is mandatory).
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Lamb shift versus dissipation]
In \cref{eq:gamma-transform} the one-sided transform of the bath
correlation function splits into a real part (rate) and an
imaginary part (Lamb shift). Explain why the imaginary part
produces a Hamiltonian term $\op H_{\rm LS}$ rather than
dissipation, and why $\op H_{\rm LS}$ commutes with $\op H_S$ after
the secular approximation.
\begin{solution}
The real part $\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ multiplies the
dissipator (the genuinely irreversible jump+anticommutator
structure); the imaginary part $S_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ multiplies
a commutator $-\tfrac i\hbar\comm{\op H_{\rm LS}}{\op\rho}$, i.e.\
unitary dynamics --- it shifts energy levels (a bath-induced
renormalisation, the analogue of the Lamb shift in atomic physics)
without causing decay. After the secular approximation only same-
frequency eigenoperators survive, so $\op H_{\rm LS}$ is built from
$\op S_\alpha^\dagger(\omega)\op S_\beta(\omega)$, which are
diagonal in the $\op H_S$ eigenbasis; hence
$\comm{\op H_{\rm LS}}{\op H_S}=0$.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Thermal bath: adding the absorption channel]
Generalise \cref{ex:amplitude-damping} to a bath at temperature $T$
by adding a second jump operator $\op L_2=\hat\sigma_+$ with rate
$\gamma_\uparrow$, while the emission rate is $\gamma_\downarrow$.
Using detailed balance $\gamma_\uparrow/\gamma_\downarrow
=e^{-\hbar\omega_q/k_BT}$, find the steady-state excited-state
population and show it is the Boltzmann factor.
\begin{solution}
The population equation becomes
$\dot\rho_{11}=-\gamma_\downarrow\rho_{11}+\gamma_\uparrow\rho_{00}$
with $\rho_{00}=1-\rho_{11}$. Setting $\dot\rho_{11}=0$ gives
$\rho_{11}=\gamma_\uparrow/(\gamma_\uparrow+\gamma_\downarrow)
=1/(1+e^{\hbar\omega_q/k_BT})$, the Fermi--Dirac/Boltzmann
thermal population. The total relaxation rate is the sum
$1/T_1=\gamma_\downarrow+\gamma_\uparrow$, and at $T\to0$,
$\gamma_\uparrow\to0$ and the qubit relaxes to the ground state as
in \cref{ex:amplitude-damping}.
\end{solution}
\end{exercise}
